🤖 Meta's AI Chief Posts a Deepfake of His Rival… and the Company's Policy Allows It!

23 November 2026 · Website · Libya Pulse

Alexandr Wang, Meta's chief AI officer, posted an AI-generated video on Instagram to promote the company's new agent 'Muse', showing him with Mark Zuckerberg, Meta's CEO, and Sam Altman, OpenAI's CEO, in a four-way video call with 'Julie', the agent's mascot.

In the clip, Altman appears as he joins the call wearing the mascot's costume: a furry hooded suit and face paint that make him look like the cute cartoon character, and Wang captioned the post about friends meeting to discuss OpenAI's product strategy — a nod to the race over AI agents and Muse's recent popularity.

The clip is entirely AI-generated, and the call never happened, but it carries a label stating it is AI-generated content, as Meta requires this label on videos depicting people. A company spokesperson said the post follows the rules, and that Altman himself allowed his likeness to be used in generated content when OpenAI launched the Sora app, which quickly filled up with fake clips of Altman.

But the rules themselves reveal a loophole: AI-generated still images require no label at all, and in October a generated image of actress Mia Goth spread on Instagram without any disclosure, from a fan account carrying the blue verification badge — and the actress's representative said allowing the platform to spread the image was 'incomprehensible', instead of focusing on protecting public figures' likenesses.

The clip is part of an unconventional promotional campaign Wang is leading for Muse, posting hundreds of posts on X, mocking traditional marketers in sarcastic terms, at a time when deepfake clips have become part of everyday life on social media — so much so that political groups have spent millions of dollars on AI-generated ads attacking their opponents before the midterm elections.

Simply put: a few years ago a fake video of a real person would spark angry statements and legal threats, but today a senior executive posts one to promote his product... Has fakery become acceptable just because it carries a small label?

If the AI chief at the biggest social-media company posts a fake video of his rival, how do you tell what is real from what is generated on your phone?
