🪐 Amateur Astronomers Discover 49 Possible Planets with the Help of AI — From NASA's Open Data!

25 November 2026 · Website · TechPulse

OpenAI revealed on November 24 two striking stories: two amateur astronomers used its AI tools to hunt for planets outside the solar system inside NASA's open telescope data, and found dozens of signals that could be new planets no one had discovered before.

In the first story, an engineer and amateur astrophotographer used the Codex tool to write a program searching Kepler telescope data for about 100,000 stars. The program pointed to more than 200 signals, including 10 planets already confirmed — proving the search worked — and then two new signals stood out around stars that were not even in Kepler's lists.

The first signal is around a cool star whose light dims every 13.8 days, which could mean a planet about 2.4 times the width of Earth, and the second is around a dimmer star that dims every 4.8 days. An astronomer at the SETI Institute repeated the search himself and found the same two signals.

In the second story, a startup founder used the Astra model to scan TESS telescope data and other public archives covering about 19 million stars. The first sweep found 49 candidates, and he sent the strongest ones to the TESS mission team for review, while two other candidates are still awaiting submission.

Importantly, there is no confirmed new planet yet: everything found so far is signals that need confirmation through additional telescope observations, which could take months or years, and a dimming star could also be caused by two stars eclipsing each other, or by a change in the telescope's own temperature.

Simply put: discovering planets is no longer reserved for major observatories — open data and AI that writes code may be enough for an amateur to find a new world from their living room.

Would you believe that amateurs without telescopes could discover planets before professional scientists?
