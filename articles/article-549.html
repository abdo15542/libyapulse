🛡️ A Space Shield Withstands Debris at 16,000 Miles per Hour — Will It Protect Astronauts?

21 November 2026 · Website · Libya Pulse

Company Atomic-6 tested a new composite shield that protects satellites from space debris, and is now investigating whether it can also protect astronauts during spacewalks. At the hypervelocity impact lab at Texas A&M University, a 3 mm aluminum ball was fired at about 7.2 km per second — equivalent to 16,100 miles per hour — toward the shield.

Placed behind the shield — a hexagonal tile of Space Armor Lite material — was a simulated model of a human torso containing artificial tissues and bones. After the impact, the model showed no visible damage, and the shield did not crack or bulge from the back. In comparison, the shield was replaced with a solid 4 mm aluminum plate, which fragments pierced and penetrated into the model — a phenomenon known as rear spallation, when fragments fly off the back face of the shield toward what is behind it.

The shield was originally designed to protect satellites from micrometeorites and orbital debris, and it completed its first space flight aboard the Starburst-1 vehicle. The tile weighs about 14 kg per square meter, and there are still major challenges before it can be used in spacesuits, including testing curved shields and different impact angles, as well as fastening, movement, and pressure-maintenance systems.

Simply put: imagine a bullet flying at 26 times the speed of sound, stopped by a light panel without cracking, while a thick metal plate shatters into fragments — that is the difference between a cleverly designed shield and ordinary metal.

Would you trust a light shield like this to protect an astronaut one day?
