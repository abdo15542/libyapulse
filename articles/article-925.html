🧠 "Meta" Launches "Uma Nitro" to Simulate Atoms Faster! And Raises Its Brain-Data Goal to 25,000 Hours!

23 November 2026 · Website · Libya Pulse

"Meta" unveiled on 20 November a new open science toolkit during the "Open Source AI for Science" summit held by its "FAIR" research team at its headquarters in Menlo Park, with researchers from universities and hospitals around the world taking part.

The headline announcement was the launch of "Uma Nitro", a faster version of the "Uma" general model for atoms, which simulates molecules and materials atom by atom before they are tested in the lab — letting scientists screen millions of possible compounds on a computer before making any of them for real.

In a joint study with Carnegie Mellon University and the University of California, Riverside, "Uma" models succeeded in identifying many crystal forms of drug molecules with no extra training, and knowing a drug's crystal form helps explain how a pill dissolves inside the body. Meanwhile, a collaboration with the University of Chicago showed the model can simulate whole enzymes atom by atom, which could help design better enzymes for medicine and energy.

It does not stop at the lab: the educational tool "Uma Playground" is now available to chemistry teachers in high schools, letting students build molecules and watch bonds form and break. The tool reaches more than 4 million teachers and 45 million students, and a joint university course between Arizona State University and Carnegie Mellon University includes it in its activities.

In the brain field, the company expanded its "Digital Brain" project, launched with the Rothschild Hospital Foundation and the University of Montreal, raising its goal from collecting 15,000 hours of brain recordings to 25,000 hours — to fund researchers who collect anonymous neural recordings and share them publicly, to understand human intelligence and treat neurological diseases.

The company also presented the open "Atlas" library, which has formalised 26 open textbooks in the "Lean" mathematical language, including more than 45,000 computer-verified definitions and results, for mathematicians and AI assistants to build proofs on without re-verifying from scratch.

Simply put: AI that tests a drug on the computer before the lab, and brain data open to everyone — is open science getting closer to speeding up the great discoveries?

Would you trust a medicine first designed by a computer simulation, or does the lab experiment remain the deciding test?
