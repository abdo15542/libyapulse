⚖️ Epic accuses Apple of hiding 122,000 documents — and demands a daily fine!

21 November 2026 · Website · Libya Pulse

Games company Epic Games accused Apple of hiding more than 122,000 documents relating to the commission Apple wants to impose on purchases made through external payment links in the App Store, and asked the court to impose a daily fine until the company hands over the documents.

Epic says Apple handed over only 632 documents from the files of seven of its senior executives, including the CEO and its new chief financial officer, while withholding more than 99% of the results on the grounds that they are irrelevant. The company adds that the delivered files contain no substantive email about how the commission rates were set, no meeting minutes, no internal financial analysis, and no App Store profit-and-loss statements.

Apple proposed in August commissions ranging from 5% to 15% on external purchases, after previously imposing up to 27%, which led the court to convict it of civil contempt for violating a court order. Epic asked the court to compel Apple to hand over all non-confidential documents matching the agreed search terms before December 2, with a daily fine for each day of delay after that.

Apple replied that the search terms Epic itself proposed are excessively broad: the word "commission", for example, catches documents about the commissioner of the American football league, and the word "fees" catches words such as "feeling", "feeding" and "feet". The company said at least 95% of the documents do not mention external payment links at all, and that they include employees' personal tax and medical records, documents about unannounced products and passwords, and it proposed instead examining a random sample of the documents by an independent expert.

Simply put: imagine a dispute over a bill, where one party demands all the other party's papers, and the second replies that most of the papers have nothing to do with the bill and some are deeply personal. The court will now decide where the right of access ends and privacy begins.
