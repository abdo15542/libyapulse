🛰️ Congress threatens to cut SpaceX funding — who slowed Ukraine's military internet?

21 November 2026 · Website · Libya Pulse

The incoming chairman of the US House Intelligence Committee threatened to cut funding for systems SpaceX develops for the government, unless the company and the US administration disclose who ordered the slowdown of Ukraine's military internet service.

Representative Jim Himes, who takes over the committee chair in January, sent a classified letter to Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton, and a separate letter to Elon Musk, both revealed on November 20.

Himes says his committee cannot fund the development of government systems that the government does not fully control, and asked for a clear explanation: what changed in the Starshield service in October 2026, why, and whether the company changed the service on its own or on orders from a government official.

Starshield is the military version of the Starlink satellite network, and Ukraine depends on it for military communications, drone operations and intercepting Russian drones.

Ukrainian officials say the company imposed on October 14 a speed limit of 161 kilometres per hour on Starshield devices, crippling interceptor drones that must fly faster than that to catch Russian jet-powered drones, and that the restrictions have remained in place for more than a week.

Musk, for his part, said on November 18 that the Ukrainian president's claim is untrue, and that Starshield's limits are decided by the US government, and that the systems are owned by the government and run by the military authorities.

Among Himes's tools when he takes the chair is a mechanism called "fencing", which lets the committee restrict funding for specific projects in the annual intelligence authorisation act until certain conditions are met, and he can also work with appropriations committees to block or redirect funding — though no restriction has been imposed yet, and it is not certain he has enough support in Congress.

Simply put: when a country's army depends on a private company for its communications, the question becomes who owns the decision — the company, the government or the army?

Should a private company be allowed to slow down a vital military service on its own?
