💻 'C++26' Survives a Historic Rejection! The Vote: 111 to 9... and 'Contracts' Stay!

23 November 2026 · Website · Libya Pulse

The C++ programming language standards committee announced on 21 November that it has completed the technical work on the new 'C++26' release for the second time, after it was rejected in the first international approval ballot — a first in the language's history.

The work had first been completed in March 2026, then the draft was put to the international vote, but five national bodies voted against it, all because of one controversial feature called 'contracts', so the draft was returned to the committee to address the comments.

The committee met this week in the Brazilian city of Búzios, attended by about 220 experts from 27 countries, and spent most of the meeting re-discussing the objections to 'contracts' — a feature that lets a programmer write conditions that must hold before and after a function runs, such as a value being greater than zero.

In the end, the committee voted by a majority of 111 votes to 9, with 8 abstentions, to resubmit the standard while keeping 'contracts' — a higher approval rate than the March vote, which was 114 to 12.

The committee also approved a new extension to 'contracts' that lets a programmer force a particular contract to always be enforced, whatever the build settings, in response to requests from the national bodies, and three other technical papers were added to the draft, concerning reflection and memory allocation.

The work did not stop at 'C++26' only: the committee also approved the first features of 'C++29', including labeled 'break' and 'continue' commands to exit specific nested loops, the 'any_view' type for ranges, and writing octal numbers with the '0o' prefix instead of a leading zero alone.

The updated draft is expected to be sent to a second approval ballot, then officially published by the International Organization for Standardization in the coming period.

Simply put: the world's most famous programming language almost failed approval for the first time, but its committee of experts saved it with an overwhelming vote... will you use 'contracts' when they reach your compiler?

Do you write conditions that the code checks automatically, or do you rely on tests alone?
