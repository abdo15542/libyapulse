🔢 The Lean community officially certifies the proof of the Riemann hypothesis with Claude's help — after checking 17 million lines!

23 November 2026 · Website · Libya Pulse

The Lean and Mathlib communities, the bodies behind the most famous mathematical proof-checking tool and its library, have issued a joint statement confirming that the proof of the Riemann hypothesis, the most famous open problem in mathematics, is correct, just one day after it was announced.

Mathematician Levent Albayrak, who works at Anthropic, published the proof on 20 November with the help of the chatbot Claude, not as a research paper but as a software library in the Lean language containing about 17 million lines spread across 175,187 files. The next day, the joint statement said: "We have full confidence that Albayrak's proof of the Riemann hypothesis is correct," endorsing both the statement and the proof.

The endorsement rested on four checks: first, the statement proved is Mathlib's own definition of the Riemann hypothesis, which Lean and number-theory experts had examined long before the proof; second, the library compiles without errors, and dozens of people recompiled it independently and obtained the same raw proof file; third, two independent tools confirmed that the theorem proved matches Mathlib's statement literally; and fourth, the exported proof was checked by the official Lean kernel and by independent checkers written in different programming languages, some of which carry a mathematical proof that they can never accept a false proof.

The hypothesis, set out by Bernhard Riemann in 1859, says that all non-trivial zeros of the zeta function lie on a single line where the real part is one half, and it governs the distribution of prime numbers. The proof also covers the generalized Riemann hypothesis, the same version for a wider family of functions. The Clay Mathematics Institute has set a one-million-dollar prize for whoever solves it.

But the endorsement does not mean anyone understands the proof yet: Albayrak himself says he is still absorbing the ideas he built, and plans to write them up in simplified form, while the community looks forward to a wave of efforts to explain the new ideas. The machine checked every step, but the human meaning of the proof is still at the very beginning of the road.

Simply put: AI helped build a proof of 17 million lines, and the computer verified it in a single day — has solving become faster than understanding it?

If a computer can prove the hardest problem in mathematics before humans understand it, what is left for the mathematician to do?
