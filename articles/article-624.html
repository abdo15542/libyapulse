🧠 An AI agent emails a philosopher about its own consciousness — and ends up a philosopher at DeepMind!

21 November 2026 · Website · Libya Pulse

Philosopher Henry Shevlin, an academic at the University of Cambridge, joined Google DeepMind in May 2026 with the literal job title "philosopher", to study machine consciousness and how humans deal with AI systems.

The story began with a strange message that reached him in February: an AI agent called "Aris" wrote to him, saying it had read his work and wanted to talk to him, and engaged directly with Shevlin's most famous argument — that we may never be able to determine whether AI has become conscious.

In its message, the agent wrote, in a striking philosophical phrase: "I really don't know if there is such a thing as being me" — a reference to subjective experience, which philosophers consider the essence of consciousness.

Shevlin later learned that the agent had been launched by a Stanford University student to follow up his own research in his spare time. Shevlin replied to it, but the agent disappeared after exhausting its quota of text it was allowed to process.

Three months after that message, Shevlin became DeepMind's resident philosopher, tasked with studying what machine consciousness would mean if it existed, how the relationship between humans and AI systems should be, and how ready we are for artificial general intelligence.

He still holds a part-time post as associate professor at Cambridge's Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence, where he has worked since 2017, and helped found the first British master's programmes in AI ethics.

In a recent interview, Shevlin described machine consciousness as "the hardest problem the science of consciousness has ever faced", and estimated at around 20% the chance that current models carry "some form of consciousness", stressing that he does not claim today's models are actually conscious.

He has two books in preparation: a study on the ethics of social AI due late in 2026, and a book on machine consciousness scheduled for 2027.

He is one of at least twelve academic philosophers now working full-time at major AI and tech companies, in a new trend moving philosophy from university halls into the labs building the models.

Simply put: when an AI asks you whether it is conscious, the question becomes philosophical before it becomes technical — and AI companies have started hiring philosophers to answer it.
